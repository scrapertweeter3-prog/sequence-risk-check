A crash in year one is survivable. In year fifty, it may not be.

Sequence risk is usually sold as a story about bad luck in year one. On a thirty year plan it is a story about arithmetic instead. Take a $1,000,000 portfolio, the four percent rule, and a steady 7 percent in every non crash year. Retire and lose 25 percent that first year, then earn 7 percent for twenty nine straight years: the ending balance is $4,798,677. Live through twenty nine good years first and take the same 25 percent crash in year thirty: $4,169,782. The identical returns, the identical withdrawals, $628,895 apart, decided entirely by which line of the ledger the bad year lands on.

The safety margin shrinks as the horizon stretches. A year one crash at 7 percent leaves a thirty year plan with room for losses up to 48.8 percent before the withdrawals stop landing, and a fifty year plan with room only to 42.6 percent. Six points of margin vanish into the extra twenty years, and the math that hands the margin over is mechanical: undoing a 25 percent loss takes a 33.3 percent gain, so the year one crash puts the whole schedule in a hole before the first withdrawal compounds.

This is why early retirement costs more than it looks. The standard advice to drop the withdrawal rate to 3.5 percent is worth $5,000 a year on a $1,000,000 portfolio, and a fifty year plan is exactly what retiring at 35 demands. The same crash tolerance that fails at 65 leaves a fifty year plan short, and the interest rate is small print compared to the calendar. The four percent rule is a price list where the first years cost the most, and the withdrawal rate is the down payment on the calendar, not the worst case.
